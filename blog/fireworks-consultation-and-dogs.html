The fireworks consultation has closed

The government's consultation on fireworks closed at 11:59pm on 7 October, and the day after, PDSA called for an end to the private use of fireworks to protect pets. Nothing will change before Bonfire Night, though, so this year it's down to us to get our dogs ready.

What the government asked

The consultation opened on 16 July. It proposed:

  • restricting noisier fireworks, so the loudest products are only available to professional users
  • adding products with "unacceptable risks" to the list of banned products
  • reviewing the rules for lower-risk fireworks to better reflect the risk they pose

The government says it is now analysing the feedback and will publish the outcome later. There's no date for a decision yet.

What the vets are saying

PDSA welcomed the recognition of the impact fireworks have on animals, but wants ministers to go further and limit private use to professionally organised public displays under clear licensing. PDSA vet Anna Ewers-Clark said: "The biggest challenge with privately used fireworks is their unpredictability." PDSA's point is that if displays are publicised in advance, owners have a better chance to adjust their routines and get help from their vet.

The British Veterinary Association made a similar case in its fireworks advice on 1 October: "Current controls on the sale and use of fireworks do not go far enough to protect animal welfare." BVA is working with the Fireworks Impact coalition, which plans an event at the Houses of Parliament on 4 November.

The rules this year

For now, the law hasn't changed. According to GOV.UK, fireworks must not be set off between 11pm and 7am, except:

  • on Bonfire Night, when the cut-off is midnight
  • on New Year's Eve, Diwali and Chinese New Year, when the cut-off is 1am

That means fireworks can go off on any evening, not just 5 November. That's the unpredictability PDSA is talking about.

Getting your dog ready now

BVA's advice for owners is to start early. In summary:

  • Talk to your vet now about options for fireworks distress, such as desensitisation, pheromone products or, where appropriate, medication.
  • Make a den, a well-padded hiding place, before the fireworks start.
  • Check the microchip, and make sure your contact details are up to date.
  • Close windows and curtains, and use background noise to mask the bangs.
  • Stay calm with a frightened dog, and never punish stress-related behaviour.

BVA lists panting, drooling and trying to escape as the obvious signs of distress in dogs, and restlessness, hiding and toileting indoors as subtler ones. PDSA's advice for dogs with severe firework fear is to contact your vet as early as possible.

Why this matters in the Forest

What follows is my own view, from walking here every day, not something PDSA or BVA said.

Around the Forest, fireworks go off over a few weeks, not on one night: village displays, garden parties, and the odd rocket on a weeknight. The clocks go back on 25 October, so the late afternoon walk will soon be in the dark.

A dog that bolts at a bang in the woods, in the dark, is very hard to find.

The Forest is big, the tracks all look the same at dusk, and a frightened dog runs a long way. I'd rather a dog never gets the chance than spend an evening searching.

What a Forest Hounds walk looks like around Bonfire Night

  • Walks happen in full daylight, well before dusk.
  • From late October until after Bonfire Night, dogs stay on a lead or long line, even ones with good recall. One bang can undo months of training.
  • I check the harness or collar fit and the ID tag before we set off, because a frightened dog can back out of a loose one.
  • One dog at a time means I can see the first signs of worry and move us away from the noise.
  • If your dog seemed unsettled, I'll tell you, so you can talk to your vet in good time.

If your dog finds fireworks hard, a quiet daytime walk keeps their routine going and takes the pressure off the evening.
