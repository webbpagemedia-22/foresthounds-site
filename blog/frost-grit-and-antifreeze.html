Winter hazards are arriving with the first frosts

As the nights get colder, three winter hazards turn up on dog walks: road grit and rock salt, antifreeze, and ice. The RSPCA, PDSA and the Royal Kennel Club all publish advice on them. Here's what they say, and how I handle it on walks in the Forest of Dean.

Grit and rock salt

PDSA says "the salt and grit we use on roads in winter can irritate pet's paws". The RSPCA goes further: if a dog swallows it, grit and rock salt can cause thirst, vomiting and lethargy, and in severe cases convulsions and kidney damage. Dogs usually swallow it by licking their paws and tummy after a walk.

The RSPCA's advice is to wipe your dog's pads, paws and tummy after every walk, and to trim the fur around the paws to stop painful ice balls forming. It also suggests dog boots to protect paws from cold ground, salt, grit and antifreeze.

Antifreeze

This is the serious one. The Royal Kennel Club says the most dangerous ingredient in antifreeze is ethylene glycol, which is also found in some windscreen de-icers, brake fluids and pond treatments. Some antifreeze smells and tastes sweet, which dogs find appealing, and dogs usually come across it through leaks from car radiators or spills when screen wash is topped up.

According to the Royal Kennel Club:

  • Soon after drinking it, a dog may be unsteady, drool or vomit.
  • Around 12 hours later, the dog may seem to recover.
  • Kidney damage can show two to three days later, with tiredness, vomiting, loss of appetite, seizures or coma, even when the early signs were mild.

Its advice is clear: contact your vet immediately if you think your dog has drunk, licked or walked through antifreeze. "Do not wait for your dog to become unwell," because treatment may work less well if it's delayed. Wash the paws with soap and water if your dog has walked through a spill. PDSA says the same: don't wait for symptoms.

Ice and frozen water

PDSA warns that slips on ice can cause serious injuries, so take extra care on walks, especially if your dog has leg problems such as arthritis. Both PDSA and the RSPCA say to keep dogs away from frozen ponds and lakes, because thin ice can break under a dog's weight. Keep your dog on the lead near them.

What I do on winter walks

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs in the Forest of Dean every day, not something the charities said. I'm a dog walker, not a vet, and nothing here replaces your vet's advice.

Most of the hazards are where cars are: gritted roads, verges, car parks and driveways. The woods themselves are mostly free of salt and antifreeze, but nearly every walk starts and ends next to a car.

If you think your dog has been near antifreeze, ring the vet straight away. Don't wait to see.

  • Keep dogs away from puddles in car parks. The Royal Kennel Club advises keeping your dog away from puddles of unusual-looking fluid. A car park on a frosty morning is exactly where those turn up.
  • Wipe paws after every walk, not just the muddy ones. A towel and a bowl of warm water by the door is enough.
  • Check your own driveway. Store antifreeze and screen wash up high, and clean up drips and spills from the car straight away.
  • Steer clear of frozen water. Forest ponds and flooded hollows freeze over in a cold snap, and a dog chasing a stick across the ice is how accidents happen.
  • Watch older dogs on icy tracks. Stiff joints and frozen ruts are a bad mix. On icy days, gentler and flatter routes are the kinder choice.
