A rescue centre on alert

The owner of a dog rescue centre in Shropshire says she is "sick" with worry after a number of suspected parvovirus cases were reported in the county, the BBC reported on 13 October. There have been no cases at her kennels, but she's taking no chances, and she's urging every owner to keep their dog's vaccinations up to date.

Shropshire isn't the Forest of Dean, and I haven't seen reports of cases here. But parvo travels on paws, shoes and leads, so it's worth knowing about wherever you walk.

What happened in Shropshire

Marty Burrell runs Hillbrae Rescue Kennels at Cold Hatton. She told the BBC she has put down disinfectant mats, stepped up cleaning, stopped taking in unvaccinated dogs, and asked people not to visit if they've been to other dog centres. An outbreak would stop her rehoming any dogs.

Her message to owners was simple: "If you don't want your dog to die of a horrible disease, then keep its vaccinations up-to-date."

What parvovirus is

According to PDSA, parvovirus is a highly contagious virus that damages the lining of the gut, causing severe vomiting and diarrhoea, and attacks the infection-fighting cells in the bone marrow. It's especially serious in unvaccinated dogs and puppies under six months old.

  • How dogs catch it. Infected dogs shed the virus in their poo. PDSA says it can survive in the environment for months to years, in gardens, parks and fields, and travel on shoes, leads, collars, bowls, toys and hands. Most dogs catch it while out exploring.
  • Signs. The BBC listed severe diarrhoea, vomiting, loss of energy, a very high or low temperature, reduced appetite, stomach pain and pale gums. PDSA says symptoms usually appear three to seven days after infection.
  • Treatment. There's no cure. Dogs need intensive care while their body fights the virus, and PDSA says parvo is nearly always fatal without treatment. The sooner a dog gets to the vet, the better its chances.

Vaccination is the protection

PDSA says most dogs are exposed to parvovirus at some point in their lives, so the only way to protect them is regular vaccination. Its vaccine guide says:

  • Puppies should ideally start at around six to eight weeks, with two injections two to four weeks apart, and are fully protected two to four weeks after the last one.
  • After the primary course, the parvo booster is usually needed every three years. Leptospirosis needs a booster every year.
  • Your vet will advise what your own dog needs.

In PDSA's 2026 Animal Wellbeing Report, 82% of dog owners said their dog had regular boosters, up from 79% in 2025. PDSA's chief veterinary adviser Steve Howard told the BBC that's encouraging but not high enough for herd immunity. He pointed out a vaccination costs less than a car's MOT and service.

Why this matters in the Forest of Dean

What follows is my own view, not something PDSA or the BBC said.

Our walks are shared. The same car parks, tracks and stiles are used by local dogs, visiting dogs and holiday dogs, every day of the year. You can't see parvo, and you can't disinfect a forest track. The one thing you control is whether your own dog is protected.

You can't keep your dog away from every germ. You can keep their boosters up to date.

  • Check the date. Find your dog's vaccination card and see when the next booster is due. If it's lapsed, ring your vet.
  • Puppies wait. Until your vet says your puppy is fully protected, keep them off shared paths and car parks.
  • Pick up, always. Parvo spreads in poo. Bagging it and binning it protects other dogs.
  • Don't wait and see. A dog with vomiting and diarrhoea, especially a young or unvaccinated one, needs a vet that day.
