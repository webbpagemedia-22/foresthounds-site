A new legal condition from 1 November

From 1 November 2026, owners of banned breed dogs, including XL Bullies, must not leave a child under 12 in close contact with their dog in a private place, such as a home or garden, without adult supervision. It becomes a condition of the dog's Certificate of Exemption, according to Defra's guidance, which applies in England and Wales.

The rule only applies to banned breeds. But Defra's new guidance on keeping children safe around banned breed dogs in the home is some of the most practical advice on dogs and children I've read, and most of it applies to any dog.

What the rule says

Defra says "appropriate adult supervision" means an adult aged 18 or over is present and able to keep children safe around the dog. The supervising adult should:

  • stay nearby and keep both the child and the dog in sight
  • pay attention to how they're interacting, and be able to step in
  • be able to separate the child and the dog straight away if needed

Supervision has to be in person. Defra is clear that cameras, baby monitors and video doorbells don't count.

If an owner doesn't follow the conditions, the dog is no longer exempt from the ban, it could be taken by the police, and the owner could be prosecuted.

The conditions owners already follow

The new rule sits alongside the existing conditions. For the life of the dog, the GOV.UK guidance says owners must:

  • keep the dog at the certificate holder's address (it can be kept elsewhere for up to 30 days in a 12-month period)
  • keep it muzzled and on a lead in public, with the lead held by someone aged 16 or over
  • keep it in secure conditions so it can't escape
  • tell Defra about a permanent change of address, or if the dog dies or is exported

The danger moments Defra picks out

Defra says young children are at higher risk of dog-related injuries at home, often from a dog they know, and that sudden movements, loud noise and close contact can make some dogs uncomfortable. It asks families to plan for the times when supervision slips, such as when an adult is:

  • cooking, or answering the door
  • on the phone or in the bathroom
  • looking after another child, or moving between rooms

It also lists times when a dog may need space: when it's resting or sleeping, eating, holding a toy, unwell or recovering, overwhelmed, trying to move away, or showing signs of discomfort.

For separation, Defra suggests separate rooms with the doors closed, secure stair gates or barriers, different floors, taking the child or the dog with you when you move rooms, or a crate or pen the dog is already comfortable in. Any barrier should stop the child and the dog touching, including reaching through or over it.

Why this matters for every dog owner

Defra says itself that many of these principles "may also be relevant to other dogs, children of different ages, and vulnerable adults". What follows is my own view, not Defra's.

The new rule is about supervision, and that's something every dog owner can do.

October half-term means grandchildren visiting, friends' children round, and a house that's busier and louder than the dog is used to. A dog that's lovely with your own children can still find a visiting toddler too much. A stair gate and a quiet room with a chew are kinder to everyone than a "they'll be fine".

A tired dog copes better, too. A good walk before visitors arrive takes the edge off, and gives the dog something to sleep off while the house is busy.

How I'd walk an exempt dog

  • At the meet and greet, I'd ask to see the Certificate of Exemption, so I know the conditions that come with the dog.
  • In public the dog stays muzzled and on a lead, which I hold, as the guidance requires.
  • One dog at a time means no other dogs to manage, and my full attention on yours.
  • The dog comes home to its own address after every walk, so the 30-day address rule isn't affected.

For any dog, a calm solo walk before a busy family day is one of the easiest ways to help them cope.
