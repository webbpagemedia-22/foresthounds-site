The clocks have gone back

The clocks went back last Sunday, 25 October, and for most of us that means the after-work walk now happens in the dark. The vet charity PDSA says that once evenings get darker you may need to walk your dog earlier, and that where you can, walking in daylight is best.

That isn't always possible, so here's what the charities and Forestry England advise, and how I think about winter walks in the Forest.

Easing your dog into the new times

Dogs don't know the clocks have changed, but they know when breakfast is late. PDSA suggests shifting walks, meals and playtime in steps of about 10 minutes a day until the routine catches up with the new time. Don't be cross about accidents while they adjust. For older dogs, or dogs with dementia, make the changes more slowly, and talk to your vet if your dog is struggling.

Being seen in the dark

PDSA vet Lynne James's advice on walking near roads as the evenings get darker is simple: "make sure drivers can see both of you." In practice that means:

  • For you: bright clothing or a hi-vis jacket, and a torch.
  • For your dog: a hi-vis coat, or an LED collar or tag.
  • Near roads: "keep them on a short, strong lead attached to a well-fitting collar when around roads."
  • Crossing: ask your dog to sit and wait, and when they're calm and the road is clear, give the command to walk and cross together.

PDSA also recommends having pet insurance in place, in case the worst happens.

What Forestry England asks

The Forest Dog Code applies all year, but it matters more when you can't see as far. It asks owners to keep their dog close and in sight, to "use a lead if they don't always return when called", and to "prevent your dog from going up to (or chasing) people or animals". It also asks you to "follow signs to avoid injury and protect forest wildlife and farm animals".

In the Forest of Dean that includes wild boar. Forestry England says dogs have been attacked and killed by boar here. It's rare, but its advice is to keep dogs "under close control and within sight, and ideally always on a lead", especially if they're unlikely to respond in woodland. If you see boar, call your dog to heel, put it on a lead and "give the animal space and if needed turn and find a different path".

Why this matters in the Forest of Dean

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs here every day, not something PDSA or Forestry England said.

A dark walk in town has streetlights. A dark walk in the Forest has none. Most of our lanes have no pavements, the verges are narrow, and drivers aren't expecting a dog walker round the next bend. Once you're under the trees, a torch only shows you a few metres of track, and a dog that runs on ahead is gone from sight in seconds.

In daylight, recall is a skill. In the dark, the lead is the plan.

  • Move the long walk to the weekend or lunchtime if you can, and keep weekday evening walks short and local.
  • Stick to routes you know in daylight. The dark is not the time to explore a new path.
  • Lead on after dusk, however good your dog's recall. You can't see what they've seen, whether that's deer, boar or sheep.
  • Charge your phone and tell someone your route if you're heading into the woods late in the day.
  • Check the lights before you go. LED collars and head torches always seem to run flat on the first cold evening.
