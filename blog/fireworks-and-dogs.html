Bonfire Night is 11 days away

Bonfire Night falls on Thursday 5 November this year, and as anyone with a nervous dog knows, the bangs start well before it and carry on well after. The British Veterinary Association (BVA) urged owners to prepare now for a season that also takes in Diwali and New Year's Eve.

This year there's a second reason fireworks are in the news: the government has been asking whether the rules should change.

Could the rules change?

The Office for Product Safety and Standards ran a consultation, Fireworks and pyrotechnics in the UK, from 16 July to 7 October 2026. It recognises "negative impacts on neighbourhood safety, vulnerable people, and animals", and its proposals include:

  • restricting "the availability of noisier fireworks to the general public"
  • adding products judged to carry unacceptable risks to the list of banned pyrotechnics
  • reviewing the rules for fireworks currently classed as lower risk

The consultation has closed and the responses are being analysed, so nothing has changed yet for this year. The vet charity PDSA used its response to call for private fireworks to be limited to licensed, professionally organised public displays. "It's much harder to protect a pet when fireworks can be set off unexpectedly, at any time and in residential areas," said PDSA vet Anna Ewers-Clark. The BVA says it doesn't think the current controls go far enough either, and is working with the Fireworks Impact coalition, which is holding an event at the Houses of Parliament on 4 November.

What vets and Dogs Trust advise

Until anything changes, it's down to us. The BVA and Dogs Trust agree on the basics:

  • Talk to your vet early if your dog really struggles. The BVA says long-term management of noise phobias can work, and options include desensitisation, pheromone products or medication where appropriate. Some medication has to be given before the fireworks start.
  • Walk in daylight. Dogs Trust suggests planning walks during daytime hours, not going out after dark, and being inside well before fireworks start.
  • Make home escape-proof. Check doors, windows and fences, and make sure your dog's microchip details are up to date.
  • Set up a den. A cosy, padded hiding place, with windows and curtains closed and music or the TV on to mask the bangs.
  • Let your dog choose. If they want to hide, let them. If they come to you for comfort, Dogs Trust says give it calmly. Ignoring them doesn't help.
  • Don't tell them off for accidents or behaviour caused by fear. Watch for the signs the BVA lists: panting, drooling and trying to escape, and the subtler ones, such as restlessness, hiding and toileting indoors.

Why this matters in the Forest of Dean

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs here every day, not something the BVA, PDSA or Dogs Trust said.

Out here, fireworks don't stay in one garden. They go up from villages all round the Forest, and the bangs carry over the woods and echo off the valleys, so a dog can't tell where they're coming from. Organised displays are easy to plan around. It's the random ones, at five o'clock on a Tuesday, that catch dogs out.

A dog that bolts in a town usually ends up on a street. A dog that bolts in the Forest can end up miles away in the dark.

  • Move walks earlier. With the clocks gone back, that means early afternoon at the latest until the season's over.
  • Lead on near villages. Even a dog with perfect recall can't hear you over a rocket, and won't stop to listen if it's frightened.
  • Check the harness. A well-fitted harness is much harder to back out of than a collar, which matters if a bang makes your dog panic and pull.
  • Keep the evening garden trip short and on a lead, even in a fenced garden. Panicking dogs find gaps you didn't know were there.
