PDSA wants an end to private fireworks

The vet charity PDSA has called for fireworks to be limited to professionally organised, licensed public displays. It set out its position on 8 October in its response to the government's consultation on fireworks.

Bonfire Night is on Thursday 5 November, less than four weeks away. If your dog struggles with fireworks, now is the time to start getting ready.

What the consultation and PDSA said

The consultation, run by the Office for Product Safety and Standards, closed on 7 October. Among its proposals was to "restrict the availability of noisier fireworks to the general public". The government is now analysing the responses, so nothing changes for this year.

PDSA wants to go further. It would keep only very low-noise products such as party poppers, sparklers and crackers on general sale. Its vet Anna Ewers-Clark said: "The biggest challenge with privately used fireworks is their unpredictability."

PDSA says fireworks set off without warning can cause fear, injuries when pets try to escape, and longer-term noise sensitivity. It argues that public displays at least let owners plan ahead and get help from their vet in time.

The British Veterinary Association made the same point earlier this month: prepare now, not on the night. It lists panting, drooling and trying to escape as the more obvious signs of distress in dogs.

Why it hits harder out here

What follows is my own view, not something PDSA or the BVA said.

In a city, fireworks are loud but expected. In the Forest villages, they're quieter most of the year, which makes the odd garden display a real shock. One back-garden rocket over Parkend or Drybrook can echo round the valley, and you rarely know it's coming. That's exactly the unpredictability PDSA is talking about.

You can't control your neighbours' fireworks. You can control when your dog is out.

Five things to do in the next four weeks

  1. Start sound work now. PDSA suggests playing firework sounds at home at a very low volume, and stopping and going quieter the next day if your dog reacts. Ideally this starts months ahead, but a few weeks still helps. Dogs Trust has free sound files, and the RSPCA recommends its Sounds Scary pack.
  2. Build a den. Pick a spot your dog already goes to when worried, cover it with blankets and add bedding. PDSA says set it up a few weeks before, give access at all times, and never shut your dog in.
  3. Walk in daylight. PDSA, the RSPCA and Dogs Trust all say the same: walk while it's light and be home before dark, when fireworks usually start.
  4. Check the microchip. The RSPCA and Dogs Trust both advise making sure your dog is microchipped with up-to-date details, in case they bolt.
  5. Talk to your vet early. If your dog is very frightened, PDSA advises contacting your vet as early as possible. Some treatments need arranging in advance, and the BVA mentions desensitisation, pheromone products and medication as options.

On the night itself, PDSA suggests closing the curtains before dark, leaving the lights on, and only taking your dog out for the toilet on a lead, in a gap between fireworks. Dogs Trust adds that a long-lasting chew can help, but to let your dog choose: a scared dog may not want to play.

How a solo walk helps

Through late October and November, I'm happy to fit walks around the light, so your dog gets a proper run in the middle of the day and is home well before dark.

  • Your dog is on the lead near villages and houses in fireworks weeks, so a bang doesn't turn into a bolt.
  • One dog means I'm watching for the small signs of stress, the yawning and panting the RSPCA mentions, not just the obvious ones.
  • A good long sniff in the woods in the afternoon makes for a calmer, more tired dog by evening.
