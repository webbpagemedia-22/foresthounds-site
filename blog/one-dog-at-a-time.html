One dog at a time: what a study of dog walkers found about divided attention

By David, Forest Hounds

When people ask why I only walk one household's dogs at a time, my answer has always been simple: I want to be watching your dog, not counting heads. A recent study from the University of Surrey puts some evidence behind that.

What the researchers did

The team spent 40 hours watching dog walkers at four sites in the Surrey Hills: Puttenham Common, the Chantries and St Martha's Hill, Norbury Park, and Denbies Wine Estate. That's roughly ten hours at each site, between April and June 2025, across mornings, lunchtimes and evenings, on weekdays and at weekends. They also held a workshop with 15 farmers, land managers, countryside business owners and conservation professionals. The study was published in the journal Society & Natural Resources.

What they found

Most of the problems linked to dog walking, such as mess left behind, wildlife disturbed and dogs getting too close to livestock, weren't down to people being deliberately irresponsible. They came from divided attention, and from bins and facilities in the wrong places.

  • Walkers distracted by their phones, by conversation or by other demands could miss their dog fouling, disturbing wildlife or approaching livestock.
  • The researchers recorded 11 occasions when dog fouling was missed. Most of it was within 50 to 100 metres of car parks and along busy paths, because dogs tend to go near the start of a walk.
  • People walking socially could get absorbed in conversation, and professional dog walkers had to divide their attention between several dogs.

The problem usually isn't bad intentions. It's attention spread too thin.

Why this matters to me

Round here we share the Forest with deer, wild boar, free-roaming sheep and plenty of other walkers. The moment that matters, a dog spotting sheep or picking something up off the path, is often over in seconds. With one household's dogs, I see it. With six on the go, I might not.

It's also why I don't fill the day. No more than three walks a day, four or five days a week, so I'm not rushing from one pick-up to the next.

Questions to ask any dog walker

Whoever you choose, these are worth asking before the first walk:

  1. How many dogs will mine be walked with?
  2. Will it be the same person every time?
  3. Where do you walk, and are there livestock there?
  4. When is my dog let off lead, and how do you decide?
  5. What do you do if my dog doesn't come back?
