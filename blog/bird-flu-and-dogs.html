A bird flu housing order now covers all of England

Since 13 November, poultry and other captive birds across the whole of England have had to be kept indoors. The UK Chief Veterinary Officer ordered the national housing measures on 11 November after a rise in confirmed cases of highly pathogenic avian influenza, or bird flu, in kept birds, and findings in wild birds. It's mainly a rule for bird keepers, but it's also a good moment to look at what it means on a dog walk.

What the government says

The Defra announcement says keepers with 50 or more birds, or who sell or give away eggs, poultry products or live birds, must house them. An England-wide Avian Influenza Prevention Zone also means every keeper, even someone with a few hens in the garden, has to follow stricter hygiene rules. The British Veterinary Association describes the order as a response to "a concerning rise" in cases in housed and wild birds over the last month.

Defra says the current risk to human health is very low.

What it means for dogs

Government guidance on keeping pets safe from bird flu says bird flu viruses can infect mammals, including dogs and cats. It describes the risk to a dog that catches an infected wild bird as small, and says common garden birds are at lower risk than ducks, geese, swans and gulls. Its advice for dog owners:

  • Keep dogs away from dead or sick wild birds, and from their droppings, feathers and carcasses. Don't let them eat, chew or play with one.
  • Keep your dog under effective control on walks so they stay away from wild birds, especially where bird flu has been found. Look out for warning posters and signs.
  • Don't feed raw poultry, game bird or wild bird meat that isn't commercial pet food.
  • Tell your vet if your dog is unwell and has been in contact with wild birds or eaten raw meat or treats.
  • Working dogs on shoots may come into contact with infected birds, including released game birds. Their owners should ask their vet for advice.

If you find dead wild birds, the government asks you not to touch them and to report them online or by phone on 03459 33 55 77. Some may be collected and tested.

What I'd take from this

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs in the Forest of Dean every day, not something Defra or the BVA said. I'm a dog walker, not a vet. If you're worried about your own dog, your vet is the person to ask.

The Forest has plenty of water, and plenty of ducks, geese and swans on it, from the ponds in the woods to the Wye and the Severn. That's where I take most care in winter. Ducks, geese, swans and gulls are the birds the guidance singles out, and a dog that loves a pond is the one most likely to find something on the bank.

A dead bird on the bank is the one thing I don't want a dog to reach first. Near water, that means a lead.

  • Lead on near water. If your dog's recall isn't reliable around birds, clip on before you reach the bank, not after you've spotted something.
  • Teach a solid "leave it". It's the most useful cue I know for anything on the ground, birds included. Practise it at home with a toy before you need it outside.
  • Don't pick it up yourself. Walk on, keep your dog with you, and report it to Defra when you're home.
  • Read the signs. If a sign says bird flu has been found, follow it, and keep away from any area it says is restricted.
  • Watch the treat bag. The guidance points out that some chews and treats are made from raw meat, and it isn't always obvious.
