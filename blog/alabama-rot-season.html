Alabama rot season has started

Alabama rot is rare, but it's serious, and the months when it's most often seen have just begun. The vet specialists who track it say dogs are more likely to develop it between November and May, and when they're walked in wooded areas after a lot of rain. That describes most of our walks in the Forest of Dean this time of year, so here's what the disease is and what I check for.

What the vets say

Alabama rot is the common name for cutaneous and renal glomerular vasculopathy, or CRGV. According to Anderson Moores Veterinary Specialists, it damages blood vessels in a dog's skin and sometimes in the kidneys. That can cause skin sores, and in some dogs it leads to sudden kidney failure.

  • It's rare. When I checked on 15 November, Anderson Moores' live map listed 335 confirmed cases in the UK since records began, one of them in 2026.
  • The cause is unknown. There is no cure yet, and no blood or urine test that confirms it. Vets diagnose it by looking at the sores and checking kidney function.
  • It doesn't seem to spread between dogs. Research so far hasn't shown it to be contagious.
  • Any dog can get it. It affects any age, but some breeds appear more likely to, including Labradors, English springer spaniels, Hungarian vizslas and whippets.
  • Time matters. Once skin sores appear, kidney failure shows up on average about three days later, although the range runs from the same day to more than 30 days. Most dogs that develop sudden kidney failure don't survive, but spotting it early may improve the chances. Some dogs only get the skin form of the disease, and their outlook is very good.

The signs to look for

The Alabama rot symptoms page suggests checking your dog over every few days after muddy woodland walks, especially in the colder months. The sores are usually on the paws or legs, but they can be on the body, face, tongue or mouth too. Look for:

  • unexplained redness
  • sores or breaks in the skin
  • swollen skin
  • more paw licking than usual

If the kidneys are affected, a dog may also be sick, go off their food or seem unusually tired. These signs can come a few days after the sores, or at the same time.

The same page is clear that most skin problems are not Alabama rot. A sore from CRGV can look a lot like a cut, sting or bite, and even when it is CRGV, many dogs don't develop kidney problems and recover fully. If you're worried, ring your vet. That's what they're there for.

What I'd take from this

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs in the Forest of Dean every day, not something Anderson Moores said. I'm a dog walker, not a vet, so treat this as a routine, not medical advice.

Lyra is a whippet, one of the breeds on that list, so this one is personal. I'm not going to stop walking her in the woods from November to May, and I wouldn't suggest you stop either. With 335 confirmed cases across the whole country since records began, the walk does far more good than the risk does harm. What I do is make the after-walk check part of the routine.

Wash the mud off, then look. Paws, legs, belly and face, every time it's been a wet woodland walk.

  • Wash, don't just towel. Anderson Moores suggests washing your dog thoroughly after every walk to get the mud off. A bucket of warm water by the door and an old towel does the job for paws and legs.
  • Look between the toes. That's where mud collects and where a small sore is easiest to miss.
  • Notice the licking. A dog that keeps going back to one paw is telling you something, even if you can't see anything yet.
  • Write it down. If you do spot something, a quick photo with the date helps your vet see whether it's changing.
  • Don't panic over every scrape. Most marks after a Forest walk are bramble scratches. The point of checking often is that you'll know what's normal for your dog, and spot what isn't.
