One dog, half an hour, one dead sheep

On Sunday 11 October a dog chased a flock of sheep for half an hour on Ardneil Farm in North Ayrshire. One sheep was so badly injured it died, and most of the 130 sheep fled onto rocks by the sea, the BBC reported. The farm had put up large signs asking people to keep dogs on a lead while the sheep were there. The two dog walkers said they hadn't seen them.

That happened in Scotland, but the same thing happens here. The Forest of Dean is one of the few places in England where sheep wander freely through the woods, along verges and into villages.

What the farmer said

Euan Jack, whose family farms Ardneil, told BBC Scotland News the sheep had been chased for half an hour before he got there. One had puncture wounds all round its neck. "We tried, but we knew it wasn't going to make it," he said.

His point was not that the dog was bad. "You hear every time something like this happens 'oh, it isn't like him, we can't believe it' and people just assume their dog won't do anything," he said. "But it's the dog's natural instinct. It's not its fault, but it needs to be on a leash."

The BBC also reported that insurer NFU Mutual estimated £330,000 worth of farm animals were killed or severely injured by dog attacks in 2025.

The law in England has changed

Scotland has its own law. In England and Wales, the Dogs (Protection of Livestock) (Amendment) Act 2025 passed in December 2025 and came into force on 18 March 2026. According to the government and Farmers Guide:

  • The maximum fine for livestock worrying is now unlimited. It used to be £1,000.
  • "Worrying" includes chasing livestock or causing them stress or panic, even if your dog never touches them.
  • The police can seize and hold a dog if they believe it could attack or worry livestock again, and can take samples and impressions from dogs and livestock as evidence.
  • The law now covers roads and paths, not just fields.
  • Courts can order the owner to pay the cost of keeping a seized dog.

The government said 87% of sheep farmers surveyed by the National Sheep Association had a dog attack on their flock in the past year.

Why this matters in the Forest of Dean

The Forest's free-roaming flocks belong to commoners, who have grazed sheep here for centuries. In 2018 the secretary of the Forest of Dean Commoners Association told the Forest Review about seven lambs attacked by dogs in Bream, Saunders Green, Parkend and Oldcroft. Five of them died or had to be put down. "We're having a serious problem with dogs being let off the lead by their owners and then attacking the lambs," he said.

Because the law now covers roads and paths, a dog that chases sheep across a Forest verge or a village green is covered just as much as one in a fenced field.

Visit Dean Wye's advice for dog walkers is to keep your dog on a short lead, no more than 2 metres, around livestock, and to keep dogs on leads anywhere near sheep. It points out that pregnant ewes can lose their lambs when frightened.

What I do, and what I'd suggest

What follows is my own view, from walking here every day, not something the BBC, the government or the Commoners Association said.

The question isn't whether your dog would chase a sheep. It's whether you'd bet a sheep's life on it.

Forest sheep turn up where you least expect them: round a bend on a track, lying in bracken, crossing a village road. A dog that ignores sheep in a field can still go after one that bolts out of the undergrowth a few metres away. And the sheep aren't the only thing that runs. Deer and boar trigger the same chase.

  • Lead on near sheep, every time. Short, not extended, and before the sheep react, not after.
  • Look ahead. On open ground and near villages, scan for sheep before you unclip.
  • Practise the boring stuff. A calm "look at me" and a reliable recall are worth more than any amount of running about.
  • If it goes wrong, stop and report it. Get your dog back, and tell the police or the sheep's owner straight away. An injured sheep left in bracken suffers.

What a Forest Hounds walk looks like around sheep

  • One dog, so I'm looking ahead for sheep, not untangling leads.
  • Your dog goes on a short lead the moment sheep are in sight, whatever their recall is like.
  • Off-lead time only happens where I can see a long way ahead and your dog's recall is proven.
