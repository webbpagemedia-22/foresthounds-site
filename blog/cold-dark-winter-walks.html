The first cold-weather alerts of the winter are out

The UK Health Security Agency issued its first cold-health alert of the winter on 10 November, for the North West and West Midlands, and a second on 14 November for the north of England, running until Thursday 19 November. Neither covers us in Gloucestershire, but the agency says to expect more as winter goes on. With dark afternoons already here, this is my winter routine for keeping dogs safe and comfortable on walks.

What the alerts are about

The UKHSA alerts are about people, not pets. A yellow alert means cold weather could affect people who are particularly vulnerable, such as those aged 65 and over or with heart or lung conditions. The agency is asking everyone to check in on vulnerable friends, family and neighbours, heat the rooms they use most, and dress warmly. That's worth remembering if you're an older dog owner yourself, or you walk a dog for someone who is.

What the vets say about dogs in winter

The vet charity PDSA's winter advice for pet owners covers the main hazards:

  • Salt and grit on roads can irritate paws. Wash your dog's paws when you get home, and a thin layer of paw butter or another pet-safe cream can help stop pads cracking.
  • Antifreeze and de-icer are very toxic to animals and can be fatal. If you think your dog has licked any, get them to a vet straight away and don't wait for symptoms. Clean up spills thoroughly.
  • Darkness. PDSA suggests LED collars, hi-vis leads and coats, and a torch for walks after sunset.
  • Cold. Some short-haired breeds, puppies and older dogs may need a coat in colder weather.
  • Ice and frozen ponds. Frozen ponds are as dangerous for dogs as for people. PDSA advises keeping dogs on a lead around water, and watching your step on icy ground.
  • Food. If bad weather means your dog is less active, they may need less food.
  • Festive plants. Holly, ivy and poinsettia are all toxic to pets if eaten.

What I'd take from this

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs in the Forest of Dean every day, not something UKHSA or PDSA said. I'm a dog walker, not a vet, so if you're unsure about your own dog, ask yours.

Lyra is a whippet, with almost no body fat and a thin coat, so winter in the Forest is something I plan for. Most of what keeps a dog safe at this time of year is simple and boring, which is exactly why it gets forgotten on a cold, dark evening.

Walk in the light when you can. Be seen when you can't. Wash the paws either way.

  • Shift walks earlier. I'd rather do the longer walk around midday and keep the evening one short and close to home.
  • Light up your dog, not just yourself. A head torch shows you the path. A light on the collar shows drivers and cyclists where your dog is, which matters on the lanes and forest roads.
  • Coat for the thin-coated. Whippets, greyhounds, older dogs and small dogs feel the cold. If yours is shivering or slowing down, it's time for a coat, or time to head home.
  • Treat the car park as the danger zone. Antifreeze drips and spills on driveways and car parks. Keep your dog close as you get in and out of the car.
  • Lead on near water when it's freezing. A dog that runs on to a frozen pond after a duck can go through the ice, and so can the person who goes in after them.
  • Warm paws, dry dog. A bowl of warm water by the door for salty or muddy paws, then a proper towel-dry before your dog settles.
