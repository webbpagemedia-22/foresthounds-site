What the vets said this week

On Friday the British Veterinary Association (BVA) launched a toolkit to help vets deal with artificial intelligence, including owners who arrive having already asked a chatbot what's wrong with their pet. It's the "Dr Google" problem, the BVA says, in a new form.

Plenty of dog owners I know already do this. So it's worth looking at what the vets actually found, and where AI fits when your dog is unwell.

The numbers

The figures come from the BVA's own Voice survey of vets:

  • Vets in clinical practice estimate that about one in seven clients (15%) use AI in their animals' care.
  • Two-thirds of the vets surveyed said clients use AI to try to diagnose an animal's health problem.
  • Owners also use it to research treatment options, check what their vet has told them, and decide whether an animal needs treatment before booking an appointment.
  • Almost half of vets said their own workplace has no AI policy.

The BVA's view is balanced. It says AI "presents important and exciting opportunities for the veterinary profession", but that it "also has the potential to cause confusion, misunderstanding and even welfare harms". The new toolkit, which is for BVA members, includes a guide to clients' use of AI and videos on how to talk to owners when a vet's diagnosis is different from what the AI suggested.

Why the "should I book?" question is the risky one

Of all the uses on that list, the one that worries me is deciding whether a dog needs treatment at all. That's the moment where a reassuring answer can cost time, and with some problems time is the thing that matters.

Poisoning is the clearest example. The vet charity PDSA's advice, repeated in its Halloween warning this week, is that if you think your pet has eaten something harmful you should contact your vet straight away rather than wait for symptoms. A chatbot can't tell you how much your dog ate, how big your dog is, or what was in it. Your vet will ask.

Where this fits on a Forest of Dean walk

Most of the things that go wrong on a walk here are the kind where waiting is a bad idea. I've written before about acorns, conkers and fungi on the forest floor in autumn, and about drugs dropped on paths and in car parks. Add a cut pad on flint or glass, a dog that comes back from the bracken limping, or one that's suddenly quiet and off its food after a day out, and you have a list of things to ring about, not type about.

What I do, and what I'd suggest

What follows is my own view, as a dog walker, not advice from the BVA or PDSA, and not veterinary advice.

Use AI to understand what your vet told you. Don't use it to decide whether to ring them.

  • Save your vet's number now, and the out-of-hours number too. Finding it at ten at night with a sick dog is the wrong time.
  • If your dog has eaten something it shouldn't, ring. Take the packet or a photo of what it was if you can.
  • Lame, bleeding, swollen, vomiting repeatedly, or just not right? Ring and describe it. Vet receptions triage calls every day, and they'd rather hear from you early.
  • Afterwards, AI can help. It's fine for explaining a word on the discharge notes or reminding you what questions to ask at a follow-up. If it says something different from your vet, ask your vet about it, rather than picking the answer you prefer.
