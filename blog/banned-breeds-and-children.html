A new rule on children and banned breed dogs

Since 1 November, owners of banned breed dogs in England and Wales, including XL Bullies, must make sure a child under 12 is not left in close contact with their dog in a private place, such as a home or garden, without an adult supervising. On 2 November the vet charity PDSA responded to the change with a reminder that applies to every dog owner, not only those with a banned breed.

What the rule says

The new condition is part of the Certificate of Exemption that every owner of a banned type has to hold. GOV.UK lists five banned types: the Pit Bull Terrier, Japanese Tosa, Dogo Argentino, Fila Brasileiro and XL Bully. Whether a dog counts depends on how it looks, not its breed or name.

The existing conditions still apply. An exempted dog must be neutered and microchipped, kept on a lead and muzzled at all times in public, and kept somewhere secure so it can't escape.

Defra published guidance on what the new condition means in practice in September. In short:

  • An adult has to be there. Someone aged 18 or over must be present and able to step in to keep children safe.
  • Close enough to act. The adult should stay nearby, keep both the child and the dog in view, and be able to separate them straight away.
  • In person. The guidance says: "Cameras, baby monitors, video doorbells or other devices are not a substitute for an adult being physically present."

It also asks owners to plan for the moments when supervision is harder, such as cooking, answering the door, taking a phone call or looking after another child. It suggests closed doors, secure stair or safety gates, keeping child and dog on different floors, or a crate or pen if the dog is already comfortable in one. In the garden, it suggests taking turns or using secure fencing to make separate areas.

The guidance lists times when a dog may need space: when it's sleeping, eating, playing with a toy, unwell, or trying to move away.

What PDSA says

PDSA's response makes a point that goes well beyond banned breeds: "Any dog, regardless of breed, can pose a risk in certain circumstances." It points owners to its own tips on keeping children safe around pets and on crate training, and to free child and dog safety resources from the Pet Education Partnership.

What I'd take from this

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs in the Forest of Dean every day, not something Defra or PDSA said. I'm a dog walker, not a behaviourist or a lawyer. If you own a banned type and aren't sure what the rule means for your household, read the official guidance in full and ask for qualified advice.

Most of the dogs I walk will never be covered by this rule. But the practical side of the guidance is good sense for any home with children and a dog, whatever the breed or size.

A dog that is eating, sleeping or moving away is asking for space. Children don't always read that, so an adult has to.

  • Give your dog a place to retreat to. A bed or crate in a quiet corner that children know is the dog's space, and that they leave alone.
  • Watch the busy moments. The school run, visitors at the door and teatime are when attention slips. A stair gate for half an hour costs nothing.
  • Don't rely on a camera. The guidance says it plainly for banned breeds, and I'd say the same for any dog: a monitor shows you what happened, not what's about to.
  • A dog that's had a proper walk is easier company. A walk won't solve everything, but a dog that has had its exercise and a good sniff is in a better place to settle when the house gets noisy.
