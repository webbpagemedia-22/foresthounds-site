Anxiety claims for dogs have nearly doubled

New pet insurance figures from the Royal Kennel Club show that claims for behavioural disorders in dogs, including anxiety-related conditions, almost doubled, up 99% between autumn 2024 and autumn 2025. With Bonfire Night on Thursday, it's a timely reminder of how hard this time of year is for a lot of dogs.

I wrote about getting through fireworks season last week. This post is about the new numbers and the last few days before the 5th.

What the figures show

The figures come from Royal Kennel Club Pet Insurance, so they describe dogs insured with them rather than every dog in the country. Even so, the pattern is clear. According to the Royal Kennel Club:

  • behavioural disorder claims were up 99% between autumn 2024 and autumn 2025
  • across 2024 and 2025, September to November had more behavioural disorder claims than any other season
  • claims in that period came to more than £110,000, with an average claim of £551

Charlotte McNamara, the Royal Kennel Club's Chief Canine Wellbeing and Engagement Officer, said: "Bonfire Night can be particularly stressful for dogs as many are sensitive to loud noises." She added that "loud noises that are tolerable to us might be uncomfortable and unpleasant to them".

What the Royal Kennel Club advises

Some of this will be familiar from last week's post, but there are a few practical extras worth knowing before Thursday. The Royal Kennel Club's advice includes:

  • Feed earlier. Give your dog their meal earlier in the afternoon, as they may refuse food once the fireworks start.
  • Top up the water bowl. Anxious dogs may pant more and get thirsty.
  • Lock the dog flap. Make sure any cat or dog flap is secured so your dog can't get into the garden unexpectedly.
  • Make the garden dog-proof, secure doors and windows, and check your dog's microchip details are up to date and that they wear a collar and ID tag.
  • Mask the noise. Keep the TV or radio on and close the curtains to block out flashes.
  • Offer a den. If you cover it with a blanket or duvet, make sure it's firmly secured and can't fall on your dog.
  • Plan walks for times when fireworks are unlikely, or keep your dog on a lead during evening walks when they are likely.
  • Never take your dog to a display, even if they seem comfortable.
  • Never tell them off for barking, whimpering or acting out of the ordinary during fireworks.

The Royal Kennel Club also says excessive yawning and panting can be signs your dog doesn't feel safe. If your dog stays very stressed despite your efforts, speak to your vet, and always check with your vet before giving your dog any medication.

For next year, it suggests gradual desensitisation: playing firework recordings at a very low volume for short periods, rewarding calm behaviour, and only raising the volume while your dog stays relaxed. That takes weeks, not days, so it's one to start in the summer rather than this week.

Why this matters in the Forest of Dean

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs here every day, not something the Royal Kennel Club said.

The claims figures don't surprise me. Autumn is a lot for a dog: darker evenings, changed routines after the clocks go back, Halloween knocks at the door, and then weeks of bangs that start before Bonfire Night and carry on long after. Out here the sound rolls across the valleys and it's hard for a dog to tell where it's coming from.

A frightened dog doesn't need a lesson. It needs a calm person, a quiet route and a lead it can't slip.

  • Walk early this week. I'd aim to be home well before dusk until the weekend after Bonfire Night.
  • Keep the lead on near villages, even with a dog whose recall is usually perfect.
  • Keep the walk boring. A familiar loop is better than somewhere new when your dog is already on edge.
  • Watch the small signs. Lip-licking, ears back, a tucked tail or a dog that suddenly wants to go home are all worth listening to.
