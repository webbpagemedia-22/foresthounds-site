New research on why big dogs age faster

A study published in Science on 8 October has found a possible reason why big dogs age faster than small ones. Researchers at Arizona State University found that the genetic "brakes" on so-called jumping genes wear off faster in larger breeds (ASU News).

It's a reminder that a big dog's "older years" can arrive sooner than you'd expect, and that's worth planning walks around.

What the study found

The team studied 864 dogs from the Dog Aging Project and mapped more than 3 million sites in their DNA where chemical tags called methyl groups help control which genes are switched on. According to the research summary:

  • Ageing went with a widespread loss of these tags, especially on transposable elements, or "jumping genes", stretches of DNA that can copy themselves around the genome.
  • One type, called LINE1, was hit hardest: more than 40% of LINE1 regions lost tags with age.
  • On average, giant breeds lost about 35% more LINE1 methylation each year than small breeds.

Co-author Blaise Mariner said that "faster aging in larger dogs may be driven, at least in part, by reduced control over these 'jumping genes.'"

What it doesn't tell us

The researchers say more work is needed to find out whether this loss of control causes ageing or is a result of it. The study doesn't offer a test for your own dog, and it doesn't come with advice for owners. What it does do is add a biological explanation to something vets and owners already know: Dogs Trust says smaller breeds tend to live longer than larger ones.

When is a dog "old"?

PDSA says dogs are, on average, considered senior at about seven years old, but that this varies with breed, size and overall health. Dogs Trust says dogs over seven can be considered "senior", still generally healthy but older, and dogs over 12 "geriatric".

Dogs Trust's advice for walking an older dog:

  • Let your dog set the pace.
  • Go for frequent short walks instead of one long one.
  • Keep them exercising: they may not want long hikes, but they still need to stay fit.

It lists signs of ageing to watch for, including less energy and more sleep, cloudy eyes, and losing the ability to hear some sounds. It says "twice-yearly veterinary check-ups are essential for an ageing dog", and to tell your vet at the first sign of behavioural changes.

What it means on Forest walks

What follows is my own view, from walking here every day, not something the researchers or the charities said.

If you've got a Labrador, a shepherd or a lurcher, don't wait for the grey muzzle. The Forest has plenty of hills, roots and steep paths, and a big dog in middle age can come home from a long hilly walk stiffer than you'd think, without ever slowing down while you're out.

A big dog will often keep up long after it should have stopped. That's why someone has to watch.

The Forest also has plenty of flatter, softer walking: wide gravel tracks, level paths, and short loops that are easy to turn back on. An older big dog doesn't need less of the Forest, just a different bit of it.

What a Forest Hounds walk looks like for an older dog

  • Your dog sets the pace, not a group. With one dog, there's nobody to keep up with.
  • I choose flatter routes and softer ground, and keep the steep climbs short.
  • Shorter walks more often, if that suits your dog better, as Dogs Trust suggests.
  • I'll tell you if I notice stiffness, slowing down, or a change in how your dog moves, so you can mention it to your vet.

If your big dog is starting to slow down, a solo walk is a good way to keep them moving without overdoing it.
