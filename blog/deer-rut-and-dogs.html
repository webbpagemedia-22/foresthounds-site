The deer rut is on: keeping your dog safe around deer

The RSPCA's reminder this week

The RSPCA has asked dog owners to keep their dogs under close control around wildlife, and to take extra care during breeding seasons. Its companion animals expert, Eloise Dicks, gave the advice in a report published on 8 October.

Here in the Forest of Dean, the breeding season that matters right now is the deer rut.

What the RSPCA said

Ms Dicks said responsible owners can help by "keeping dogs under close control, particularly around nesting birds and other vulnerable animals, and taking extra care during the breeding season."

She also made a point I like: dog walkers are out in all weathers and are "often well placed to spot pollution, injured wildlife or changes to local habitats", and can report what they see.

The same report notes that the rut generally runs from late September to early November, when male red and fallow deer move around more and can behave more aggressively. The RSPCA's own deer guidance puts the wider window, across all species, at mid July to December, and says that if you walk your dog where deer live, your dog should be on a lead.

Deer in the Forest of Dean

Visit Dean Wye lists three species to look out for in the Forest of Dean and Wye Valley: fallow, roe and muntjac. Fallow are the ones you're most likely to see.

A few things it says are worth knowing as a dog owner:

  • Fallow bucks get bolder during the autumn rut, when they're carrying their full antlers.
  • You're more likely to hear the rut than see it.
  • A buck's grunt can sound like a wild boar.
  • Deer turn up along road edges, and sometimes in the middle of the road.

Forestry England's deer-watching guide says deer are most active at dawn and dusk. Its advice for people with dogs is plain: keep your dog in close control on the lead if you meet deer, give deer space, and stay on main tracks and paths, especially during the rut.

Why it matters for your dog

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs in the Forest every day, not something the RSPCA or Forestry England said.

Most dogs that chase deer never catch one. The danger is in the chase itself. A dog locked onto a running deer stops listening, crosses ground it doesn't know and can end up a long way off, or on a road. In the rut there's a second problem: a buck with his mind on other bucks may not run at all.

A chase that lasts ten seconds can take you an hour to sort out.

Sighthounds like Lyra are the obvious worry, but I wouldn't trust any dog's recall against a deer breaking cover a few metres away. Spaniels, terriers and collies all have their moments too.

Roads are the other half of this. The RSPCA's autumn advice asks drivers to take care at dawn and dusk and to watch for deer during the rut. The forest roads that cross our walking routes are exactly where a startled deer, or a dog following one, can end up. The same page suggests a reflective collar for dogs now the evenings are drawing in.

If you find an injured deer

Don't let your dog near it, and don't try to move it yourself. The RSPCA asks people to call it on 0300 1234 999 if they find an injured, trapped, entangled or sick deer.

How I walk during the rut

One dog, one walker, so I can see what's ahead as well as what's at the end of the lead. Between now and the end of the rut:

  • Your dog is on the lead in the woods wherever deer are likely, and always near roads.
  • We stick to the main tracks, as Forestry England asks, and keep clear of thick cover.
  • I walk in daylight, not at first light or dusk, when deer are most active.
  • If we hear grunting or groaning in the trees, we turn round. Deer or boar, the answer is the same.
  • I'll tell you if we met deer and how your dog coped, so you know what to expect on your own walks.

If your dog has a strong chase instinct, a solo walk with training is a good way to build a "leave it" and a reliable recall on the lead first, before anything is tested in the open.
