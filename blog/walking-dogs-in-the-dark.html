The evenings are dark again

The clocks went back on 25 October, and for a lot of owners the after-work walk now happens in the dark. In the Forest of Dean that can mean unlit lanes, narrow verges and woodland tracks that look very different once the sun has gone. Here's what the vet charities advise, and how I'd handle it here.

What the vet charities advise

PDSA's winter walking advice starts with the simplest step: "Where you can, try to walk your dog during the daylight hours." When that isn't possible, PDSA and the RSPCA recommend:

  • Be seen. The RSPCA suggests sticking to well-lit paths and using reflective or LED gear for both you and your dog, so drivers can see you. PDSA mentions reflective gear and LED collars.
  • Carry a torch. PDSA calls a good torch "a must-have to light the way for you both".
  • Keep the lead on. PDSA says: "Don't let your dog off the lead after dark - there could be hidden hazards out there you can't see."
  • Keep the microchip up to date. The RSPCA says this matters in case your dog slips away in bad weather.

Near roads, PDSA vet Lynne James has advised keeping dogs "on a short, strong lead attached to a well-fitting collar", using a reflective collar that's a "quick release" type so it can't get caught on anything, asking your dog to sit and wait before you cross together, and making sure drivers can see both of you.

What the dark changes in the Forest

What follows is my own view, from walking dogs in the Forest of Dean every day, not something PDSA or the RSPCA said.

Plenty of the lanes between our villages have no pavement and no streetlights, and the traffic on them doesn't always slow down. In the dark, a dog in a dark coat is close to invisible from a car until the last moment.

If a driver can't see your dog, they can't avoid it. Light up the dog, not just yourself.

  • Light the dog, not just you. A head torch shows you the path, but a light-up collar or a reflective coat is what shows a driver there's a dog beside you.
  • Choose a route you know. After dark isn't the time to explore a new track. Roots, ditches and the odd bit of litter are far harder to spot.
  • Expect wildlife. Forestry England warns that deer collisions on forest roads are more common at dawn and dusk. A dog that catches the scent of deer in the dark is a dog you want on the lead.
  • Mind older and thin-coated dogs. PDSA says dogs with thinner fur, and older or unwell dogs, may feel the cold more. A coat helps, and a reflective one does two jobs.
  • Don't let the walk shrink too much. A dark evening makes it tempting to cut the walk to ten minutes round the block. Do that for a whole winter and most dogs will show it.
